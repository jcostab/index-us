An AI announcement usually tells you what a team wants the market to notice. It rarely tells you everything a buyer, builder or operator needs to know.

The useful question is not “is this impressive?” It is “what, precisely, can I rely on?”

## 1. Find the primary source

Start with the vendor documentation, model card, repository or research paper. A social post and a launch video are discovery tools, not specifications. Record the announcement date and the date you checked it.

## 2. Confirm actual access

“Available” can mean public, waitlisted, region-limited, enterprise-only or accessible through a research preview. Note the plan, country, interface and account requirements. Those details determine whether readers can reproduce the result.

## 3. Separate the model from the product

A model capability does not guarantee that every product surface exposes it. Context windows, file types, tool use and safety settings often differ between an API, a consumer app and an enterprise product.

## 4. Read the limits before the highlights

Look for rate limits, file limits, retention policies, excluded use cases and known failure modes. The limits page usually changes the practical meaning of the headline.

## 5. Reproduce a narrow claim

Choose a task with an observable result. Keep the input, settings and output. Run it more than once when the product is probabilistic. A single successful demo is evidence of possibility, not reliability.

## 6. Check data handling

Establish whether prompts or files may be retained or used for training, and whether controls differ by plan. Do not put sensitive material into a new service merely to test it.

## 7. Publish what remains uncertain

Good coverage names the gaps. If access is unavailable, say the article analyses an announcement rather than a hands-on test. If pricing or rollout details are missing, do not fill the silence with assumptions.

This method is deliberately unglamorous. It is also how a fast-moving feed becomes a trustworthy index.
