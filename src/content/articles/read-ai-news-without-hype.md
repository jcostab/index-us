AI news mixes research, product marketing, policy, investment and speculation in one continuous stream. Reading more of it does not automatically produce a clearer view.

A compact set of questions helps.

## What changed?

Name the actual change in one sentence. A new model shipped, a price moved, a feature reached general availability, a benchmark improved or a rule took effect. If the change cannot be stated plainly, the story may be mostly interpretation.

## What is the evidence?

Separate a peer-reviewed result, a vendor benchmark, a controlled demonstration and an executive forecast. Each can be informative; they do not deserve the same confidence.

Look for the comparison baseline. “Twice as fast” is incomplete without the earlier system, workload, hardware and quality target.

## Who is affected now?

An important research result may have no immediate product impact. A minor API change may matter greatly to teams running it in production. Identify the user, region, plan and timescale.

## What would change your mind?

Write down the missing evidence: independent replication, public access, a complete price sheet or tests outside the demo domain. This prevents a confident narrative from hardening before the facts arrive.

## What should a reader do next?

Most news needs no immediate action. Useful actions are specific: read the migration guide, test one workflow, review a data policy, monitor a rollout or simply wait for evidence.

The goal is not cynicism. It is calibrated attention—enthusiasm that survives contact with the source.
